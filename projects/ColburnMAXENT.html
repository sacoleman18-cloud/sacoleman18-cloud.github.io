Oral Presentation

Utilizing MAXENT to determine the top covariates related to Myotis Activity in central Wisconsin

Collected bat data from a local wildlife management area in Central Wisconsin to model Myotis activity using MAXENT.

Lead Author
← All Projects

Abstract

Myotis includes two resident bats in Wisconsin, the little brown bat (M. lucifugus) and the northern long-eared bat (M. septentrionalis), both of which are state threatened, with the northern long-eared bat being federally listed under the Endangered Species Act (ESA). The biggest conservation issues that these Myotis face are the invasive fungal disease white-nose syndrome, habitat fragmentation, and insecticide use. Due to the state and federal listing of these species, land managers must adapt their land-management techniques to work around the active presence of these bats. The habitat selection of Myotis is an under-researched topic that deserves more attention due to their listings. Our research is looking at the top environmental covariates that lead to the presence of these bats. We placed microphones in randomly generated locations throughout the 20-km2 Colburn Wildlife Area, a state-owned preserve in central Wisconsin that contains a variety of biomes, including aspen wetlands and oak-dominant woodlands. We also conducted variable radius plots at each microphone location and recorded various data on each “in” tree. Each variable radius plot’s data was then interpolated to create a raster file which encompassed our study area. We used a multimodal approach utilizing the principle of maximum entropy and the software MAXENT, to determine the highest weighted environmental covariates related to Myotis activity. This software utilizes a jackknife cross-validation technique to determine the importance of each variable. We chose to implement a bootstrap resampling technique to test the strength of our model. The MAXENT model determined that the tree diversity is the only covariate that has a significant effect on M. lucifugus. M. septentrionalis activity was explained by tree plot’s basal area and average tree decay stage. The model strength for M. lucifugus and M. septentrionalis is slightly better than random and high performing respectively.

Key Findings

  • Only our oak-aspen homogenious scale was significant in explaining M. lucifugus activity, while
  • M. septentrionalis activity was explained by basal area and average tree decay stage.
  • Custom filters assigned to each AudioMoth detector yielded lower quality calls, which thus probably resulted in fewer calls being detected.

Methodology

We deployed 4 AudioMoth bat detectors in randomly generated locations throughout the Colburn Wildlife Area, cycling each detector around once every 2 weeks from June 1st 2024-August 31st. Detectors were filtered to only detect Myotis species to save space on microSD cards. We then conducted variable radius plots at each detector location and recorded various data on each “in” tree, including each tree's diameter as breast height (DBH), presence or absence of peeling bark, an ordinally assigned rank to each tree's canopy class with 1 being suppresed and 4 being dominant, each tree species, and a categorically assigned number based on each tree's decay stage on a scale of 1-5 with 1 being the most decayed and 5 being the least decayed. Each variable radius plot’s data was then interpolated to create raster files for our study area. We extracted Bat calls from each AudioMoth detector utilizing Kaleidoscope Pro. We used a multimodal approach utilizing the principle of maximum entropy and the software MAXENT, to determine the highest weighted environmental covariates related to Myotis activity. This software utilizes a jackknife cross-validation technique to determine the importance of each variable. We chose to implement a bootstrap resampling technique to test the strength of our model.
